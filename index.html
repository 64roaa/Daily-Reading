Meet Sam

    ودود

    /ˈnɜː.vəs/
    متوتر
  • different (adj)
    /ˈdɪf.ər.ənt/
    مختلف
  • nurse (n)
    /nɜːs/

Hello! My name is Sam and I am thirty years old. I am from Canada, but now I live in London. I work in a small office near the city centre. I am a designer. My flat is not big, but it is quiet and clean. I live with my cat, Milo.

Today is Monday, and it is my first day at an English class. The teacher is a friendly woman. Her name is Mrs Brown. There are twelve students in the class, and they are from different countries. I meet a man from Egypt. His name is Ali, and he is a nurse. We are both nervous, but we are happy to be here. After class, we have coffee together.

GRAMMAR FOCUS
The verb “to be”

Use am with I, is with he / she / it, and are with you / we / they. Add not for the negative. Put the verb first to make a question.

  • I am a designer.
  • I am not from Spain.
  • Is Ali a nurse?

  3. 3.What is Ali’s job?

My friend Hana and I share a flat. We get up at six thirty every weekday. First, we take a shower and get dressed. Then we make breakfast. We usually have toast, eggs and a cup of tea. We don’t watch TV in the morning. We talk about our day and check the weather on our phones.

At seven forty-five, I take the bus to the office. The journey takes thirty minutes, so I listen to music or read a book. I arrive at eight fifteen. I always say good morning to my colleagues. Then I turn on my computer and start work. I like my morning routine because it is calm and simple.

GRAMMAR FOCUS
Present simple (I / you / we / they)

Use the base verb with I, you, we and they. Use don’t for the negative. Time words like every day, usually and always show a routine.

  • We usually have toast and tea.
  • We don’t watch TV in the morning.
  • I take the bus to the office.

Every Friday morning, Mrs Noor goes to the market with her basket. The market is busy and noisy, but she likes it. She buys fresh vegetables and fruit there. A man sells red tomatoes. A woman sells yellow lemons and green mint. Mrs Noor knows many sellers, and they know her.

She stops at a small stall. The seller smiles and says, ‘Good morning! Do you want apples today?’ She asks, ‘How much are they?’ He answers, ‘Three dollars a kilo.’ That is a good price, so she buys two kilos. He puts the apples in her basket. She says thank you and walks home. At home, she makes a big fruit salad for her family. Everyone loves Fridays.

GRAMMAR FOCUS
Present simple (he / she / it)

Add -s to the verb with he, she and it: she buys, he sells. Use goes, does and has for go, do and have. Use doesn’t for the negative.

It is a rainy Saturday, and Tom is at home. He wants to go for a run, but he can’t. The sky is dark and grey, and the rain is falling hard. Tom is looking out of the window. ‘I’m bored,’ he says. His girlfriend, Sara, is in the kitchen. She is making soup, and the smell is delicious.

Now Tom is sitting on the sofa with a book. Sara is listening to music. The cat is sleeping next to them. Outside, people are running with their umbrellas. Lina has a small white cat. Its name is Snow. One morning, Snow is not in the flat. Lina looks under the bed, but there is no cat there. She looks in the kitchen. There are two bowls on the floor, but there is no cat. Lina feels worried. She goes outside and asks her neighbour, ‘Is there a white cat in your garden?’

‘Yes, there is,’ says the neighbour. ‘There is a cat on the roof of the garage.’ Lina looks up. Snow is there! The cat looks afraid and cannot come down. A man from the next house brings a long ladder and carefully takes the cat down. Lina holds Snow in her arms. ‘Thank you so much,’ she says. Now Lina always closes the window before she goes to work.

GRAMMAR FOCUS
There is / There are

Use there is with one thing and there are with more than one. Use there isn’t / there aren’t for the negative. Ask: Is there …? Are there …?

  • There is a cat on the roof.
  • There are two bowls on the floor.
  • Is there a white cat in your garden?

Omar is a student at a language school in the evening. He works in a shop during the day, so he is often tired. In class, he can read and write well. He can’t speak fast, but he is learning. His friend Salem can speak English very well. The teacher says, ‘Salem, you can help new students with speaking.’

At break time, the students drink tea in the corridor. Some of them can play the guitar, and some can sing. Omar can’t sing, but he can cook! He often brings sweet cakes for everyone. After the break, there is a speaking test. Omar is nervous, but he can answer all the questions. ‘I can do it,’ he thinks. After school, he goes home happy and sleeps early.

GRAMMAR FOCUS
Can / can’t

Use can for ability: He can read. Use can’t when you are not able to do something. The verb after can has no -s: She can sing.

  • Omar can’t sing.
  • Can you play the guitar?

Yara is my best friend. She is tall and thin, with long black hair and brown eyes. She wears round glasses and a big smile. She is clever and funny. She works as an engineer in a large company. Her smile is warm, and everybody likes her.

Every morning, Rami waits at the bus stop. It is opposite a small shop and next to a bank. There is a tall tree behind the bench. Rami sits on the bench and puts his bag between his feet. He looks at the clock above the shop. It is half past seven. The bus is late today.

An old woman stands in front of him. She has a heavy bag, so Rami gets up and offers her his seat. ‘Thank you, young man,’ she says with a smile. At last, the bus arrives. Last summer, my sister and I visited the beach with our parents. We travelled for two hours by car. When we arrived, I jumped out and looked at the sea. The water sparkled in the sun. And can we have extra pillows?’ ‘Of course,’ smiled the clerk. ‘Would you like a wake-up call?’ The tired family nodded, thanked her and followed a hotel worker to the lift. The room had a wonderful view of the sea.

GRAMMAR FOCUS
Polite requests

Use Could you …? or Can I / we …? to ask politely. Use Would you like …? to offer something. Add please to sound more polite.

  • Could you check again, please?
  • Can we have extra pillows?
  • Would you like a wake-up call?

The Hidden Room

Dina and her brother Zaid were cleaning their grandmother’s old attic when Zaid noticed something strange. One of the walls sounded hollow. ‘Knock on it,’ said Dina. Knock, knock. ‘There must be an empty space behind it,’ said Zaid. ‘It can’t be solid.’ ‘It might be a secret door,’ Dina whispered. They pushed a heavy wardrobe aside and found a small wooden door with a rusty handle.

‘What might be inside?’ asked Zaid. ‘Maybe old toys, or maybe treasure,’ Dina joked. Together they turned the handle, and the door opened slowly. Inside, there was a tiny room with a dusty desk, a lantern and a pile of letters tied with a ribbon. ‘These must belong to Grandmother,’ said Dina. They carried the letters downstairs. Grandmother smiled when she saw them. ‘I wrote these to your grandfather,’ she said, ‘a long time ago.’ That evening, she told them the story of her first love.

GRAMMAR FOCUS
Must, might and can’t (guessing)

Use must when you are almost sure something is true. Use might when it is possible. Use can’t when you are almost sure something is not true.

  • It might be a secret door.
  • There must be a space behind it.
  • It can’t be solid.

The Long Journey

Before the sun came up, Mariam and her father left their small town and started a long journey to the capital. They were visiting Mariam’s aunt, who lived 800 kilometres away. ‘We should leave early,’ said her father, ‘so we can avoid the traffic.’ Mariam packed some snacks, a blanket and her favourite book. They drove for three hours and then stopped at a quiet petrol station for a rest.

After breakfast, the road climbed through high mountains. Mariam looked out of the window and counted the tunnels. While her father was driving, she read aloud, and he listened with a smile. Late in the afternoon, the sky turned orange, and the lights of a big city appeared in the distance. ‘We are almost there,’ said her father. When they finally arrived, the aunt was waiting outside and waving with both hands. Mariam was tired but happy. After a long journey, she knew that family is worth the trip.

GRAMMAR FOCUS
Linking time words

Use before, after, while, when and until to link two events. Use a comma when the time clause comes first: After they ate breakfast, the road climbed.

  • Before the sun came up, they left home.
  • While her father was driving, she read aloud.
  • When they arrived, the aunt was waiting.
